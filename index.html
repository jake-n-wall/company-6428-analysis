Product and Marketing Analysis – Company 6428

A product strategy case study for a tax software company: picking a launch bundle from customer research, spotting the next roadmap opportunity, and questioning a marketing budget that the data couldn’t justify.

  • Context MKTG 6428 course project
  • Inputs Survey of 150 customers (2,700 product ratings); 4 years of weekly sales
  • Methods Conjoint analysis, regression

What I recommended

  1. Launch Product B (DIY software + on-demand expert, $120, audit support). It has the highest predicted rating of the three options (6.86 out of 10, vs. 6.02 for A and 5.61 for C), based on a conjoint model of 2,700 customer ratings.
  2. Scope a “Do it for me” bundle with audit support as the next roadmap bet. Customers value full-service filing most, and Product A lost only because it was overpriced and missing audit support.
  3. Treat audit support as a core feature and hold the price at $120. Preference drops three times faster between $120 and $130 than between $110 and $120.
  4. Run an advertising holdout test before the next tax season. About 63% of the annual ad budget goes to Q1, yet once seasonality is accounted for, ad spend shows no measurable effect on sales.

The problem

Company 6428 sells tax preparation software and could launch only one of three new product bundles. Leadership also wanted to know what actually drives weekly sales, and whether advertising and social media were pulling their weight. The bundles differed on three attributes:

ProductService levelPriceAudit support
ADo it for me (an expert files for you)$130No
BDIY + on-demand expert$120Yes
CDIY software only$110Yes

I approached it the way I’d approach a launch decision: first understand what customers value, then turn that into a choice among the options on the table, then check whether the levers the business was already pulling (ads, social posts, price, reviews) were moving sales.

How I approached it

I worked in three phases. First, I tested leadership’s assumption about advertising by starting with a simple regression and adding fiscal quarter, price, reviews, and social posts one at a time to see whether the answer held. Second, I ran a conjoint analysis on the survey to measure how much each feature and price point moves customer ratings. Third, I turned both into decisions: scoring the launch options, comparing what each dataset says about price. All models were run in Radiant (R).

Each insight below links to the model output behind it.

Insight 1: Product B has the highest predicted rating

To compare the three options on equal footing, I used the conjoint model to predict a rating for each one. The model uses all 2,700 survey ratings to estimate how much each service level, price point, and feature adds to or subtracts from a baseline product (DIY, $110, no audit support, rated 4.53). Because each attribute’s effect is estimated separately, the model can score any combination of features, including products customers never saw side by side.

A product’s predicted rating is the baseline plus the value of each of its features:

The table shows why B wins. It is the only option that combines a service upgrade with audit support while keeping the price penalty small. Product A has the most valuable service level (+2.318), but the $130 price takes back more than a third of that gain, and it lacks audit support (+1.078). Product C has audit support and the lowest price but no service upgrade, so it adds the least to the baseline.

Decision: Launch Product B. It has the highest predicted rating of the three options, 0.84 points ahead of the next best option.

Insight 3: Audit support is a must-have, and $120 is the price ceiling

Two findings shape packaging and pricing:

  • Audit support is worth more than a $20 discount. Adding it raises the rating by 1.08 points, more than the 0.83-point gain from cutting the price from $130 to $110. It belongs in the core bundle, not as an upsell.
  • Price sensitivity accelerates past $120. Moving from $110 to $120 costs 0.20 rating points; moving from $120 to $130 costs a further 0.63, roughly three times as much. That points to a psychological threshold between $120 and $130.

The sales data supports price mattering: each $1 increase in average price is associated with about 640 fewer units sold per week. But because that model fits a straight line, it can’t reveal the threshold. Only the survey, which measured each price point separately, shows where customers start pushing back.

Decision: Include audit support in the launch bundle and hold price at $120. Test anything higher before rolling it out.

Insight 4: The advertising budget may be funding demand that already exists

At first glance, advertising looks like a strong growth lever: weeks with more ad spend sell more, and ad spend alone explains 85% of the variation in weekly sales. Leadership was effectively making budget decisions on that assumption.

The problem is timing. Ad spend averages about $775K per week in Q1 (January–March, tax season), versus $135K–$181K in the rest of the year. That puts roughly 63% of the annual ad budget in a single quarter, the same quarter when customers need to file and sales peak regardless.

Advertising and sales were both high in Q1 for the same reason: it’s tax season. The original read credited the ads for demand the calendar created.

Social media posting showed the same pattern: no measurable effect on sales in any model.

This doesn’t prove advertising is useless. Because spend rises and falls with the season, the historical data simply can’t separate the two. That’s a measurement gap, and it’s fixable.

See the regression output

Model 1, ad spend alone. The coefficient of 0.116 means 116 more units per extra $1,000 (p < .001, R² = 0.845).

Model 2, adding fiscal quarter. The ad spend coefficient falls to 0.003 (p = 0.783) and R² doesn’t move from the quarter-only model (0.895).

Supporting model: fiscal quarter explains 94.3% of the variation in ad spend, which is why the two can’t be separated.

Decision: Before next tax season, run a geographic holdout test: pause or reduce ads in a set of matched markets and compare sales against markets running the normal plan. With a Q1 budget around $10M, even a small test could prevent a large misallocation or confirm the spend is working.

Insight 5: What actually moves sales: season, price, and reviews

A model with fiscal quarter, price, and average review rating explains 92.6% of the variation in weekly sales. Ad spend and social posts add nothing on top of that. The two levers the business controls that do matter:

  • Reviews: each additional star in the week’s average rating is associated with about 18,400 more units sold. A 0.1-star improvement is worth about as much as a $2.90 price cut.
  • Price: each $1 increase is associated with about 640 fewer units per week.
See the regression output

Adding price and review rating raised adjusted R² from 0.893 to 0.924. Both are significant at p < .001.

Adding social media posts left R² unchanged at 0.926; the posts coefficient was 0.018 (p = 0.932).

Decision: Treat product quality and review volume as growth levers, for example by improving the post-filing review prompt and prioritizing fixes behind low ratings. Test price changes carefully, since each $1 increase costs about 640 units a week.

What I’d do next

  1. Validate with real behavior. Survey ratings capture stated preference. A pricing page A/B test or a limited launch of Product B would show whether customers actually buy the way they said they would.
  2. Size the full-service opportunity. Partner with finance and operations to estimate the cost per return of a “Do it for me” bundle and see whether it can be profitable at $120.
  3. Run the ad holdout test and define success upfront: incremental units per $1,000 spent, compared against a target cost per acquisition.
  4. Segment customers. Understanding who those customers are (for example, by return complexity) would sharpen targeting and positioning.

Limitations

  • Stated vs. revealed preference. Survey ratings may not match real purchase behavior, and results only apply to the prices and features tested.
  • No cost data. The analysis ranks options by customer appeal, not profitability.
  • Correlation, not causation. The sales models show associations. Only an experiment can establish what advertising actually causes.
  • Simulated course data. The company and datasets were provided for a class, so the findings illustrate the approach rather than a real market.
